\begin{abstract}
Task-oriented dialogue increasingly relies on small language models (SLMs) that
emit structured output (intent + slots) via autoregressive generation. We show
that for \emph{closed-set} intent detection and slot filling this is both slow
and brittle, and unnecessary. We present a recipe that converts a generative SLM
into a \textbf{discriminative single-pass} model: (1) a frozen linear-probe
sweep over a small predefined depth grid $\{L/4, L/2, 3L/4, L\}$ identifies an
aggressive candidate pruning region \emph{a priori}; (2) a linear intent head
and a token-level slot head (softmax or CRF) replace token-by-token
generation. On a 124M-parameter decoder, pruning to 3 of 12 layers yields
approximately 5\,ms P50 CPU latency versus approximately 359\,ms for the
autoregressive baseline on ATIS ($\sim$70$\times$ speedup) while eliminating
a generative parse-failure rate of up to 46.7\% (0\% by construction). At a
matched 3{,}000-example budget, depth 3 matches depth 12 at 96.08\% intent
accuracy on ATIS while reducing P50 latency from 20.4 to 5.4\,ms. Fine-tuning
at candidate depth 3 preserves most downstream task performance across ATIS,
SNIPS, MASSIVE, CLINC150, and BANKING77 while substantially reducing
inference latency relative to full depth. A diagnostic dense sweep (layers
$1{-}12$) reveals that linear accessibility of intent information precedes
fine-tuning sufficiency by several layers---a cheap intent probe alone
under-selects depth on the shallow end, and a token-level slot probe on
SNIPS mirrors that under-selection. We further report a negative-results
study of CPU inference optimizations: dynamic INT8 and \texttt{torch.compile}
give no benefit for shallow models, while ONNX Runtime yields a $4.5\times$
speedup at the cost of brittle export.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Small language models are increasingly deployed for task-oriented natural
language understanding (NLU), where a user utterance is mapped to a discrete
\emph{intent} and a set of typed \emph{slots}. A common pattern is to fine-tune
a generative SLM to emit a structured object (e.g.\ JSON
\texttt{\{"intent":...,"slots":...\}}) autoregressively. This inherits two costs
from generation: (i) latency that scales with output length, and (ii) a nonzero
rate of \emph{malformed} outputs that fail to parse into the target schema.

We argue that closed-set intent + slot filling does not need generation. Our
central claim: \emph{converting a generative SLM into a probe-pruned
discriminative single-pass model yields a large CPU speedup and zero
structured-output parse failures. Frozen probes identify an aggressive
candidate pruning region a priori, but linear accessibility of task
information precedes fine-tuning sufficiency by several layers; the final
deployed depth is confirmed by downstream fine-tuning, not by the probe
alone.}

\textbf{Contributions.} Two primary contributions:
\textbf{(C1)} \emph{Probe-guided candidate pruning}: a frozen linear probe
over a small predefined depth grid identifies an aggressive candidate region
before fine-tuning, reducing the need to exhaustively train every depth.
Dense diagnostics further reveal a failure boundary where linear task
accessibility precedes fine-tuning sufficiency; on the slot-bearing SNIPS
benchmark a token-level BIO-tag probe exposes this gap.
\textbf{(C2)} \emph{Generative$\rightarrow$discriminative conversion}: a large
speedup plus a reliability guarantee (parse-failure rate $\rightarrow$ 0 by
construction). Supporting analyses cover a measured implicit-slot scope
statement (0\% on our benchmarks) that bounds where span-based heads suffice,
and a study of common CPU inference optimizations that shows the systems win
comes from architecture (prune + classify), not post-hoc kernel/quant tricks.
We position our contribution honestly: against the generative baseline the win
is decisive; against a purpose-built bidirectional encoder we are competitive,
and our recipe requires no separate encoder---it reuses an existing decoder.

\textbf{Why a decoder?} Our target setting is one in which an existing causal
decoder is already available and must be adapted for latency-sensitive
closed-set NLU. The goal is therefore not to outperform purpose-built
encoders, but to determine how much of an existing decoder is actually
necessary and to eliminate autoregressive decoding when the output space is
closed and structured.
\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Structured NLU.} Discriminative intent + slot prediction has a long
lineage; JointBERT~\citep{chen2019bert} and successors fine-tune an
encoder~\citep{devlin2019bert,vaswani2017attention} with an intent classifier
and a token-level tagger. Constrained decoding~\citep{willard2023guidance}
restores validity for generative structured outputs but retains autoregressive
cost; discriminative heads make validity structural at no decode cost.

\textbf{Efficient transformer inference.} Layer dropping~\citep{fan2020layerdrop},
dynamic early exit~\citep{xin2020deebert}, distillation~\citep{sanh2019distilbert},
and rotation-based quantization~\citep{ashkboos2024quarot} all target inference
cost. Closer to our setting, recent decoder-only depth-pruning
work~\citep{men2024shortgpt,gromov2024unreasonable} removes LLM layers using an
\emph{unsupervised} block-similarity or influence signal, then heals the
result with light fine-tuning; we instead use a \emph{supervised} linear
probe over task labels on a small predefined depth grid, and---unlike that
line of work---explicitly test and find cases where the probe signal itself
is misleading (\cref{sec:results}). We use \emph{static} depth pruning guided
by frozen probes, complementary to and stackable with kernel/quant work (\S6).
The upstream trend toward small
language models~\citep{allal2024smollm,abdin2024phi3} makes the
\enquote{how much of the decoder do we actually need} question
increasingly practical for on-device
NLU.

\textbf{Probing.} Prior probing~\citep{alain2017probes,tenney2019bert} primarily
analyzes \emph{where} information is encoded in a pretrained network. We
instead operationalize frozen probing as a pre-fine-tuning signal for static
decoder truncation, and characterize an important limitation: linear
task-information accessibility can precede downstream fine-tuning sufficiency
at extreme shallow depths, so a probe alone does not identify the deployed
depth. A substantial literature already cautions against reading probe
accuracy at face value: it can reflect probe capacity rather than
representation content~\citep{hewitt2019control}, ignore extraction
difficulty~\citep{voita2020mdl}, or decode information the model does not
causally use~\citep{elazar2021amnesic}; see \citet{belinkov2022probing} for a
survey. Our failure mode is distinct from all of these and complementary to
them: we hold the probe fixed and ask whether its accuracy at layer $\ell$
predicts the accuracy of a model \emph{truncated at $\ell$ and then
fine-tuned}---a deployment-time question that decodability, extraction cost,
and causal-use critiques do not address. We find it does not, with negative
within-dataset rank correlation on four of five benchmarks. Concurrent
work~\citep{ibanezlissen2025lpass}
similarly uses frozen probes before fine-tuning to pick a layer-pruning
cutoff, but in a different domain (source-code vulnerability classification),
probing a proxy structural signal (code-complexity metrics) rather than the
task label itself, via a dense per-layer sweep rather than a predefined sparse
grid with a tolerance rule; critically, that work treats probe accuracy as
validated evidence for the cutoff, whereas we show probe accessibility can
\emph{over-recommend} an aggressively shallow depth that then underperforms
after fine-tuning.

\textbf{Setup.} A closed intent set $\mathcal{I}$ and BIO slot tags
$\mathcal{T}$; we predict one intent and a tag per token in a single forward
pass. \textbf{(3.1) Probe-guided candidate pruning.} We freeze the pretrained
decoder and, in one forward pass with hidden-state output, mean-pool the hidden
states at a \emph{predefined sparse candidate grid} of depths
$\{L/4, L/2, 3L/4, L\}$ over non-pad tokens. A linear
probe (logistic regression) is trained on each depth's pooled features to
predict the intent. We select the shallowest depth whose probe accuracy is
within $\epsilon$ of the best (we use $\epsilon=0.02$). \emph{This grid is
chosen a priori, not tuned to the data.} A dense per-layer sweep
($\{1,\ldots,L\}$) is used as a diagnostic in \cref{sec:results} to test the rule's limits,
not as the deployment procedure. The probe is therefore used to identify a
candidate pruning region rather than to guarantee the final downstream
optimal depth. \textbf{(3.2)
Discriminative heads.} We truncate the backbone to the selected depth, add a
linear intent head over mean-pooled hidden states and a per-token slot head
(softmax cross-entropy, or a linear-chain CRF~\citep{lafferty2001crf} with
Viterbi decoding). The joint loss is
$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{intent}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{slot}}$
with $\lambda=2$; we length-normalize the CRF NLL so it does not dominate the
intent term. \textbf{(3.3) Reliability.} Because output is a fixed set of
classifier decisions, it is structurally valid: parse-failure rate is 0 by
construction.

\textbf{5.1 Probe-guided candidate pruning (C1).} Over the predefined sparse
grid $\{3,6,9,12\}$ the ATIS frozen probe scores 95.0/95.4/95.3/96.0\%; the
$\epsilon{=}0.02$ rule selects depth 3 (within 1pt of full). All five datasets
independently return depth 3 on this grid. Fine-tuning at depth 3 matches or
beats full depth on intent accuracy on ATIS/SNIPS while being $\sim$4$\times$
faster. To test the probe as a \emph{predictor} rather than just a decision
rule on this grid, we compute Spearman's rank correlation between frozen probe
accuracy and fine-tuned intent accuracy \emph{within each dataset} (the metric
aligned with C1). Across the six evaluated depths $\{1,2,3,6,9,12\}$ per
dataset, per-dataset
$\rho$ is $-0.612$ (ATIS), $+0.353$ (SNIPS), $-0.754$ (MASSIVE), $-0.486$
(CLINC150), $-0.771$ (BANKING77); mean $-0.454$, median $-0.612$. \textbf{The
frozen intent probe does not rank-order depths within a dataset.} The pooled
cross-dataset $\rho{=}+0.871$ we report elsewhere reflects between-dataset
difficulty (easier tasks have higher probe \emph{and} higher fine-tuned
accuracy), not within-dataset depth ranking. Reported honestly, frozen intent
probes reliably identify an \emph{aggressive candidate region} for pruning
but do not reliably rank fine-tuned depths within individual datasets---the
final deployed depth is confirmed by downstream fine-tuning.

\textbf{Does linear accessibility imply fine-tuning sufficiency?} No. The
dense frozen probe of layers $1{-}12$ (\cref{tab:denseprobe}) shows
intent representations are already highly linearly accessible at
\emph{layer 1} on every dataset (ATIS 95.2\%, SNIPS 96.7\%, MASSIVE 74.4\%,
CLINC150 87.6\%, BANKING77 81.9\%). Under the $\epsilon{=}0.02$ rule the
dense sweep would select depth 1 for all five datasets. Yet downstream
fine-tuning at depth 1 (\cref{tab:d1vsd3}) loses $2.7$--$4.1$pt of
intent accuracy on the fine-grained benchmarks and substantially more slot F1
(up to $10.9$pt on SNIPS). On ATIS and SNIPS, depth-1 intent accuracy is at
most $3.3$pt below depth 3. Depth 2 recovers much of this gap---within
1.73pt of depth 3 on intent across all five datasets---while depth 3 is the
shallowest \emph{evaluated} fine-tuned depth (among $\{1,2,3,6,9,12\}$) that
consistently preserves both intent accuracy and slot F1. Frozen intent
probes over-report depth-1's usability; the substantive negative finding is
that linear accessibility of task information precedes fine-tuning
sufficiency at the extreme shallow end, and cheap intent-only probes cannot
detect the gap.

\textbf{Slot probes reveal what intent probes miss (on SNIPS).} We repeat the
dense layer sweep with a token-level BIO-tag probe (frozen backbone, linear
tag classifier per layer) on the three slot-bearing datasets
(\cref{tab:slotprobe}). ATIS and MASSIVE slot signal is nearly flat with
depth (within $\sim$1pt from L1 to L12), yet their downstream depth-1 slot F1
still drops by $4.5$ and $2.9$pt respectively---so the flat slot probe on
these two does \emph{not} explain their smaller collapses. SNIPS, in contrast,
shows a sharp $\mathbf{+7}$pt jump from L1 (77.4\%) to L2 (84.5\%), then
plateaus; that L1$\to$L2 transition mirrors the downstream depth-1 SNIPS slot
F1 collapse (82.2 at depth 3 $\to$ 71.3 at depth 1). We take this as
single-dataset evidence that \emph{task-aligned} token probes can reveal a
phase transition that intent-only probing hides, and as motivation for a
slot-probe-aware selection criterion: pick the shallowest layer that jointly
satisfies the intent- and slot-probe $\epsilon$-rules. On SNIPS that
refinement would select depth 2 instead of depth 1 and restore
method-experiment consistency; we present it as a promising refinement of C1
rather than the paper's headline rule.

\textbf{Matched budget control.} To isolate pruning depth from training
budget, we train depths 1, 3, and 12 on the same 3{,}000 ATIS examples.
Depths 3 and 12 achieve identical intent accuracy of 96.08\%, while P50
latency falls from 20.4 to 5.4\,ms. Depth 1 reaches 92.83\% intent accuracy
at 1.8\,ms, confirming that the extreme shallow setting introduces a
measurable capacity cost even at matched budget.

\textbf{5.2 Reliability (C2).} Generative parse-failure rate tracks slot-schema
complexity: 46.7\% on ATIS (135 BIO tag labels; the M1 baseline run itself

\textbf{5.3 CRF ablation.} With a length-normalized CRF loss, the CRF slot head
gives a small consistent gain over softmax at depth 3 (ATIS $+0.19$, SNIPS
$+0.27$, MASSIVE $+0.09$ F1); it does not close the gap to the bidirectional
encoder, suggesting that transition modeling alone does not explain the gap.
Attention directionality and other encoder-specific properties (pretraining
objective, tokenizer, model family) remain possible explanations.

\textbf{Slot-loss weight $\lambda$.} We sweep $\lambda \in \{1.0, 1.5, 2.0,

middle of a smooth curve---no cliff, no sharp optimum. The main conclusions
are insensitive to $\lambda$ within this range.

\cref{tab:negresults} summarizes four conditions on the same weights.
\textbf{Dynamic INT8} gives no benefit: at depth 3 the Linear layers are not
the bottleneck, per-batch quant/dequant overhead offsets the cheaper matmuls,
and the path silently fails to run on a stock CPU build without
\texttt{torch.backends.quantized.engine = "qnnpack"}---a portability footgun.
\textbf{\texttt{torch.compile}} is marginally \emph{slower}: compile overhead
and dynamic sequence shapes prevent graph fusion from amortizing at this scale.
\textbf{ONNX Runtime} yields a real $4.5\times$ speedup at identical accuracy,
but the modern dynamo exporter fails on the decoder and the legacy exporter
rejects the dataclass output; it only succeeds after wrapping the model to
return a plain \texttt{(intent\_logits, slot\_logits)} tuple. Takeaway: for
shallow closed-set NLU the win came from \emph{pruning + discriminative
single-pass}, not post-hoc kernel/quant tricks; ONNX graph execution is an
orthogonal, stackable win with real export friction.

\textbf{Implicit-slot scope (measured, not estimated).} A slot value is
\emph{implicit} if it is not a contiguous span of the utterance; span-based
taggers cannot recover it by construction. We measured the implicit-slot rate
on all three slot-filling benchmarks and found it is exactly \textbf{0\%}
(ATIS: 0/16{,}350; SNIPS: 0/37{,}542; MASSIVE: 0/14{,}159 gold slot values).
These benchmarks are span-annotation-first by design, so our span-based heads
have no theoretical recall ceiling here; tasks with a genuine implicit-slot
rate (e.g.\ coreference- or ellipsis-dependent slot filling) would need a
generative fallback or explicit non-span recovery rules, which we leave to
future work rather than force onto benchmarks that do not exhibit the
phenomenon. This is a scope-validation result, not an independent contribution.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
Closed-set assumption; open-set/novel-intent detection is out of scope.
Generation is still needed for free-form tasks. The representation
accessibility-versus-fine-tuning-sufficiency result is established here for a
single 124M causal decoder on a single CPU; whether the same depth behaviour
generalizes to newer decoder families or larger models remains an open
question. The fine-grained gap on BANKING77 shows depth genuinely helps for
many same-domain intents---probe-guided candidate pruning trades a small,
quantified accuracy cost there for a $4\times$ speedup.

\textbf{Fairness caveats on baselines.} Our generative baseline is bounded to
3{,}000 train / 300 eval examples because autoregressive CPU decoding is
expensive; the discriminative rows use 5{,}000. The latency and parse-failure
comparisons remain apples-to-apples (they are architecture properties). The
matched budget control in \cref{sec:results} isolates pruning depth from training budget:
depths 3 and 12 reach identical intent accuracy at 3{,}000 examples,
confirming that pruning depth-12$\to$3 is an architecture win, not a
training-budget confound.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Generate less, classify more: cheap frozen probes identify an aggressive
candidate pruning region, downstream fine-tuning confirms a shallow operating
depth, and discriminative heads deliver fast, reliable, single-pass intent
and slot prediction from an existing generative SLM.
